# Slide cognitive-rhythm patterns

Use these patterns to redesign a section without manufacturing drama. Choose the pattern that matches the material and talk contract; do not apply every pattern.

## Cognitive modes

- **Orient** — establish the current problem, scope, or decision.
- **Observe** — show a fact, incident, measurement, code result, or diagram before interpreting it.
- **Question** — expose a real gap between expectation and observation.
- **Explain** — supply a mechanism, definition, or causal step.
- **Test** — apply the explanation to an example, counterexample, demo, or constraint.
- **Contrast** — compare alternatives on one explicit axis.
- **Decide** — name a choice, boundary, accepted cost, or criterion.
- **Apply** — transfer the decision to the audience’s work.
- **Reflect** — revisit the opening after the model has changed.
- **Pause** — let a consequential fact or decision settle without adding a second task.

Mode names are planning labels. Do not print them on slides unless they are meaningful domain labels.

## Material constraint

Build a shift only from material the deck can support:

- verified events, records, measurements, code, and source quotations;
- a familiar expectation contradicted by evidence;
- benefits and costs of real alternatives;
- a speaker-provided decision, revision, failure, or remaining uncertainty;
- a fair audience question implied by stated prerequisites.

When no such material exists, keep the explanation direct. A flat truthful section is stronger than artificial suspense.

## Section entry

Open a section with one of these relations:

- a concrete the previous conclusion cannot explain;
- a fair objection to the previous claim;
- an alternative the speaker genuinely considered;
- a term that names a pattern the audience has already seen;
- a decision now possible because the prerequisite is complete.

Avoid a transition slide that only says what comes next. A transition earns time when it changes the audience’s task, restores orientation after a long section, or marks a real decision boundary.

## Density waves

After several slides that require code reading, table comparison, or multi-step inference, shift to one content-bearing function:

- state the fact the audience can now treat as settled;
- show the one relationship the details established;
- test the model on a concrete counterexample;
- name the decision the evidence enables;
- zoom from instance to system or from system back to instance.

Do not create a breathing slide by restating the preceding heading. Sparse slides still need a state change.

## Projected text and speech

- Put durable structure, exact syntax, numbers, and comparison axes on the slide.
- Put connective explanation, caveats, and emphasis in speech only when losing one sentence will not break later understanding.
- Do not ask the audience to read dense prose while the speaker explains a different claim.
- Repeat a key term when it restores orientation; do not repeat a full argument merely to slow down.

## Lists, tables, and diagrams

Land a classification in one of four ways:

- identify which item explains the opening example;
- connect a row or node to a concrete datum or code line;
- use the comparison to make or reject a decision;
- state the condition where the model stops applying.

Do not follow a table with a prose slide that says the same thing in different words.

## Questions and payoff

Give every opening question, assumption, demonstration promise, and recurring example a payoff location. Partial answers may accumulate, but each step must add content rather than announce progress. End by returning to the opening concrete and showing what the audience can now see or decide differently.

Leave an issue unresolved only when the deck established why the uncertainty is real. Do not hand an unfinished argument to the audience as “food for thought.”

## Limits

Reference decks, workshops, runbooks, and hands-on procedures may benefit from predictable repeated structure. Prefer retrieval, safety, and exercise completion over dramatic variation. Apply cognitive rhythm mainly at transitions between tasks, not inside every instruction.
