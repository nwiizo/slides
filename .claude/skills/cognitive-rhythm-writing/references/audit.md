# Slide cognitive-rhythm audit

Use this sequence for diagnosis and after edits. Report logic, facts, comprehension, or duration problems under their owning review rather than disguising them as rhythm findings.

## State-update test

For every sparse slide, section opener, transition, standalone question, and punchline, ask what changes:

- **World state** — a new event, datum, code result, constraint, or relationship appears.
- **Audience model** — a mechanism, distinction, boundary, or counterexample changes understanding.
- **Decision state** — an option becomes available, rejected, conditional, or costly.
- **Deck state only** — the slide announces sequence, summarizes progress, or requests attention without new content.

Keep the first three when they serve the talk. Delete or rewrite deck-state-only material unless it provides necessary navigation or recovery.

## Navigation that may remain

- the opening audience promise;
- an agenda required by the event or a long modular talk;
- a section marker after an exercise, break, demo, or major scope change;
- instructions that affect how the audience performs a task;
- an explicit marker that an example is hypothetical or a claim remains unverified.

“Next we will,” “so far we have seen,” and “let us return” are not automatically wrong. Keep them only when the audience would otherwise lose a real boundary or recovery point.

## Audit order

1. **Contract** — confirm audience, duration, central question, answer, and intended decision.
2. **Completeness** — ensure rhythm edits did not remove scope, comparison axes, prerequisites, qualifications, or uncertainty.
3. **Mode map** — label the audience task for each slide or short range; flag long runs with no content-grounded shift.
4. **Question ledger** — pair questions, assumptions, and promises with payoff slides. Remove unsupported suspense.
5. **Density wave** — mark code, table, diagram, prose, and sparse runs together with speaking burden and planned time.
6. **State updates** — apply the state-update test to transitions, punchlines, and pauses.
7. **Speech split** — find slides that demand close reading while speech carries a separate argument.
8. **Recovery** — test where the audience can reorient after missing one slide, a code step, or a definition.
9. **Non-fabrication** — trace every added incident, reaction, quote, metric, decision, and uncertainty to the deck or user input.
10. **Regression** — compare facts, causality, boundaries, Marp syntax, asset paths, duration, and adjacent transitions before and after edits.

## Symptom to repair

- **Every slide has the same texture** — identify repeated audience tasks; change mode only where a concrete, test, comparison, or decision already exists.
- **Correct but hard to stay with** — locate the real unresolved question and its payoff. Do not insert unrelated questions.
- **Theory drains attention** — show the concrete discrepancy the theory names before the definition, unless the talk is intentionally reference-first.
- **Lists do not stick** — connect the items to the opening example, a comparison axis, a choice, or a boundary.
- **Sparse slides feel empty** — apply the state-update test; delete decorative pauses or give them one earned consequence.
- **Dense slides feel rushed** — change the speaking task, split at a conceptual boundary, or keep the slide on screen longer; do not solve it with smaller text.
- **Transitions feel mechanical** — remove deck narration and let the next concrete, objection, or decision create the bridge.
- **Ending feels generic** — return to the opening material and state what model or decision changed without introducing a new thesis.

## Severity

- **HIGH** — the central question loses tension or payoff, a long mode run hides the decision, or pacing makes the core argument unusable live.
- **MEDIUM** — repeated deck narration, unlanded lists, weak recovery, or avoidable density flatten a section.
- **LOW** — a local beat, handoff, or pause can improve without changing the section’s function.

Never require a finding count or numeric rhythm score. Preserve sequences whose repetition supports practice, accessibility, emphasis, or deliberate calm.
